How this works

Matchup data comes from Trainer Hill. The filter at the top of the page shows the window. Trainer Hill collects and publishes the tournament data. This page only gives it a different weight.

The expected win rate for a deck is the average of its matchups. Each matchup gets the weight of that opponent's meta share. The page then divides by the total share that you enter. An example: the field is 40% Dragapult and 60% Gardevoir, and your deck wins 45% against Dragapult and 55% against Gardevoir. The expected win rate is (0.4 × 45 + 0.6 × 55) / 1.0 = 51%.

Your shares do not need to total 100%. The page scales them. Be careful what that means: it drops the rest of the field, and it does not treat the rest as neutral. Add the Other bucket to stand in for every deck you did not list.

Ties are a choice, not a fact. This format is best of three, so a tie often works out as a loss for whoever needed the win. Settings offers all three readings: ignore ties, count them as losses, or count them as half a win. One matchup can move three points between them, so pick the one that matches your event.

Regression to 50% is why a 3–0 matchup does not show as 100%. Most matchups have very few games. Across all 130 decks, the median matchup has only two games. The page therefore pulls each matchup toward even by a prior worth k pseudo games: (W + k/2) / (W + L + k). At the default k = 20, a 3–0 record reads as 56%. A 60–40 record over 100 games hardly moves. Set k to 0 in Settings to see the raw numbers. This also solves the empty matchups: two thirds of all deck pairs have no recorded games, and with no evidence they sit at exactly 50%.

The interval is the honest part of the answer. That same prior is a Beta distribution, so each matchup carries a variance as well as a mean. The page adds those up across the matchups, weighted by your shares, and reports a 95% interval. A rule in the table separates groups that the data can tell apart. Inside a group, the order is noise, and a deck one place higher is not a better choice. The interval covers the games played. It does not cover your shares being wrong, which is usually the larger risk.

Your own deck is for a brew with no tournament record. You give it a win rate against each deck in the field, and you say how much testing stands behind those numbers. An estimate backed by 30 games enters the arithmetic as an 18–12 record, so it regresses toward 50% exactly as a real record does. An untested deck therefore cannot climb the ranking on assertion alone, and the page shows what each estimate counts as after regression. Give it a meta share and it becomes an opponent too: every other deck then faces it at the complement of your numbers, because a matchup you win 60% of the time is one they win 40% of the time.

The page shows the sample size everywhere. A thin label marks a matchup below 20 counted games. Treat a thin matchup as a hint, not as a measurement.
