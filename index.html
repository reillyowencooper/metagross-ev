How this works

All of the matchup data here comes from Trainer Hill, which does the genuinely hard work of collecting and publishing tournament results — this page just reweights it. The filter up at the top tells you exactly which tournaments are in the pool.

A deck's expected win rate is its win rate into every deck you listed, weighted by how much of the field you think each of those decks will be. Say you expect the field to be 40% Dragapult and 60% Gardevoir, and your deck wins 45% into Dragapult and 55% into Gardevoir. Your expected win rate is (0.4 × 45 + 0.6 × 55) / 1.0 = 51%.

Your shares don't need to add up to 100% — we'll scale them for you. Be careful about what that actually means, though: scaling throws the rest of the field away, it doesn't treat it as neutral. If you list the top eight and they come to 60%, you're asking how you'd do at a tournament where those eight decks are the entire meta. Add the Other bucket to stand in for everything you didn't list.

Ties are a judgment call, not a fact. We're playing best-of-three, so a tie usually works out as a loss for whoever needed the win. I default to ignoring ties entirely, but Settings will let you count them as losses or as half a win instead. A single matchup can swing three points between those three readings, so pick whichever one matches the event you're prepping for.

Regression to 50% is why a 3–0 matchup doesn't show up as 100%. Most matchups in this data are thin — across all 130 decks the median pairing has all of two decisive games — so we pull every matchup toward even by a prior worth k pseudo-games: (W + k/2) / (W + L + k). At the default k = 20 that 3–0 reads as 56%, while a 60–40 over 100 games barely budges. Drop k to 0 in Settings if you'd rather see Trainer Hill's raw numbers. This handles the empty matchups for free, too: two thirds of all deck pairs have never actually played each other, and with no evidence either way they sit at exactly 50%.

The interval is the honest part of the answer. That prior is a Beta distribution, so every matchup carries a variance as well as a mean, and we add those up across matchups to get a 95% interval. The table draws a rule wherever the data can actually tell two decks apart — inside a group the ordering is noise, and the deck sitting one row higher is not the better pick. Read the interval, not the decimal. One caveat worth saying out loud: the interval covers the games that got played. It doesn't cover your meta shares being wrong, which is usually the bigger risk.

Your own deck is there for a brew that doesn't have a tournament record yet. You give it a win rate into each deck in the field and tell me how much testing is behind those numbers. An estimate backed by 30 games goes into the math as an 18–12 record, so it regresses toward 50% exactly like a real record does — an untested deck can't just declare itself good. Give it a meta share and it becomes an opponent as well: everything else then faces it at the complement of your numbers, since a matchup you win 60% of the time is one they win 40% of the time.

Sample sizes are on display everywhere. A thin label means there are fewer than 20 games behind that matchup, so treat it as a hint about the matchup rather than a measurement of it.
