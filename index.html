How this works

All the matchup data comes from Trainer Hill, which collects and publishes the tournament results that everything here sits on top of. This page only reweights what they already gathered. If you want to know which tournaments went into the numbers, the filter at the top of the page spells it out.

A deck's expected win rate is its win rate into every deck you listed, weighted by how much of the field you think each one will be. Suppose you expect 40% Dragapult and 60% Gardevoir, and your deck wins 45% into Dragapult and 55% into Gardevoir. That works out to (0.4 × 45 + 0.6 × 55) / 1.0 = 51%.

Your shares don't need to add up to 100%, since I scale them for you. Before you trust the number, think about where the missing slice went. It gets dropped. So if you list the top eight and they come to 60%, your answer describes a tournament where those eight decks are the whole field and the other 40% never shows up. Put the Other bucket in and that 40% becomes an opponent again.

How a tie counts is your call. In best-of-three, a tie hurts whoever needed the win. My default throws ties out of the calculation entirely, though Settings will count them as losses or give them half credit if you'd rather. One matchup can move three points depending on which you pick, so choose based on the event you're getting ready for.

A 3–0 matchup won't show up as 100%, because I pull every matchup toward even before using it. Most of what Trainer Hill has is thin. Across all 130 decks the median pairing has two decisive games in it. So each matchup gets a prior worth k pseudo-games: (W + k/2) / (W + L + k). That 3–0 reads as 56% once k is 20, which is the default. A 60–40 built on 100 games moves by less than a point. Set k to 0 in Settings and you'll see Trainer Hill's raw numbers. The same prior covers the pairs with no games at all, and two thirds of all deck pairs have never played each other. With nothing to go on they sit at 50%.

That prior is a Beta distribution, so every matchup carries a variance next to its mean. Add those up across a deck's matchups and you get the 95% interval in the table. When two decks' intervals stop overlapping, I draw a rule between them, and that rule is the only place the ranking tells you something. Inside one block the order is noise, and a deck one row up isn't a better pick. The interval only knows about games that were played. Whether your meta shares are any good is a separate question, and getting those wrong will cost you more than sampling error will.

Your own deck handles a brew with no tournament record. Give it a win rate into each deck in the field, then tell me how much testing sits behind those numbers. An estimate backed by 30 games enters the math as an 18–12 record and regresses toward 50% the same way a real record would, so typing 70% into everything won't get you to the top. If you also give it a meta share, it starts playing the other side: every other deck faces it at the complement of what you entered, because a matchup you win 60% of the time is one they win 40% of the time.

Sample sizes show up throughout the page. A thin label means fewer than 20 games went into that matchup. At that size you can read which way a matchup leans and very little else.
